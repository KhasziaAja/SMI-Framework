### 5.1 Global Demographic Phase Transition ($P_{net} < 1.0$)

High birth rates in developing economies were sustained not by moral duty, but by economic structure: children were net contributors to household survival ($P_{net} > 1.0$)[cite: 4, 5]. 

As global urbanization, digital connectivity, and statutory schooling spread, children become net financial liabilities ($P_{net} < 1.0$) across developing nations as well[cite: 4, 5]:
* **Thailand:** TFR $\approx 1.16$ (below Japan)[cite: 2, 5]
* **Urban Vietnam:** TFR $1.20 - 1.30$[cite: 2, 5]
* **India:** National TFR down to $2.0$ (below replacement level); Tier-1 urban centers (Delhi, Bengaluru) at $1.20 - 1.40$[cite: 4, 5]

The global pool of replacement labor is drying up[cite: 2, 4, 5]. **Importing young populations is a temporary arbitrage against a rapidly depleting global supply**[cite: 2, 4, 5].

### 5.2 Sending-Nation Political Incentives

Developing states permit outward labor flows because of short-term institutional benefits[cite: 4, 5]:
1. **Foreign Exchange Remittances:** Capital flows totaling 20% to 40% of domestic GDP support weak sovereign finances[cite: 4, 5].
2. **Safety Valves for Youth Unemployment:** Exporting underemployed cohorts relieves domestic political unrest and insurgency risks[cite: 4, 5].
3. **Currency Arbitrage on Domestic SMI:** Workers earn wages in strong currencies and deploy them where the denominator is small, artificially boosting home-nation SMI without domestic institutional reform[cite: 4, 5].

Once sending states urbanize, this currency arbitrage closes[cite: 4, 5]. Jurisdictions that neglect domestic cohort reproduction (SMI) in favor of external labor imports will find themselves stranded once global competition for human capital peaks[cite: 2, 4, 5].
